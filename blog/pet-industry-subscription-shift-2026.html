84.6% of Chewy's Sales Are on Autopilot — What the Pet Subscription Shift Means for Suppliers

By Bonnie Woo | September 12, 2026 | 6 min read

Two signals dropped on September 9 — same day. Chewy reported Q2 net sales of $3.33 billion, up 7.3% year over year, with 21.7 million active customers. And Reuters reported that Golden Pet Brands — the employee-owned company behind Dr. Marty Pets and Badlands Ranch — has hired Morgan Stanley and Barclays for a New York IPO at a valuation above $1 billion.

Put the two together, and a clear picture emerges for anyone on the sourcing side: the pet industry is no longer just growing — it is restructuring around subscription demand and DTC brands. Here's what that means for suppliers.

1. Pet Demand Isn't Discretionary Anymore — It's Subscription Infrastructure

The number that matters in Chewy's earnings is not the topline growth. It's this: 84.6% of sales came from Autoship subscriptions — recurring, locked-in, predictable revenue.

📊 Key Data Point

Chewy Q2 net sales: $3.33B (+7.3% YoY) | Active customers: 21.7M | Autoship share of sales: 84.6%

When 85% of the category leader's revenue runs on autopilot, demand forecasting gets radically more predictable — for Chewy, for its vendors, and for everyone downstream in the supply chain. Pet food, treats, litter, toys, and accessories stop behaving like discretionary retail and start behaving like infrastructure. That's good news for every manufacturer in the chain: steadier order flow, fewer demand shocks, and more rational capacity planning.

2. The Next Wave of Pet Brands Won't Look Like Your Current Clients

DTC brands like Dr. Marty Pets grew up on Instagram storytelling, not shelf placement. And they buy differently:

  • Smaller opening orders — they test before they scale
  • Faster iteration — seasonal drops, limited editions, rapid reformulations
  • Obsessive about packaging and unboxing — the box is part of the product
  • Content-calendar speed — they expect their supplier to move at the pace of their posting schedule, not a traditional buying cycle

For suppliers built around big-box retail POs — large volumes, annual planograms, fixed specs — this customer profile can feel chaotic. But it's not chaos; it's a different operating system. And the suppliers who learn it early will lock in relationships with the brands that define the next decade.

3. Capital Is Already Pricing This Shift

Golden Pet Brands brought in a former Mars and P&G executive as CEO in April to prepare for the public markets. A freeze-dried pet food platform with no legacy retail network is now worth $1B+ to public market investors.

The buyers of the next decade are being built right now — and they need suppliers who can do small-batch, fast-turn, brand-sensitive production.

When institutional capital validates the DTC pet model at that valuation, it triggers a wave: more founders, more funding, more new brands looking for manufacturing partners. The question for suppliers is not whether this wave is coming — it's whether your factory is legible to these buyers when they arrive.

What This Means for OEM Suppliers

The suppliers who only know how to serve big-box retail POs will find the next generation of pet brands hard to work with. The ones who learned small-batch flexibility will find them to be the best customers they've ever had. Concretely, that means building:

  • Low-MOQ, small-batch production capability — profitable runs that let brands test and iterate
  • Fast-turn sampling and reorders — weeks, not quarters
  • Brand-sensitive manufacturing — custom packaging, private label, retail-ready presentation out of the factory
  • Subscription-ready consistency — when your client's revenue is 85% recurring, a quality slip doesn't cost one order; it costs a subscriber
